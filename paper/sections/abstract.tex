\begin{abstract}
The standard move in multi-agent systems is to weight messages by source
reliability. We argue this answers the wrong question whenever reliability
and communication convention are distinct latent variables. When agents
report through \emph{source-specific conventions}, mappings from internal
readings to messages that can invert the signal or transform it depending
on context, the real difficulty is interpreting the message, and no
reliability weight can fix a misinterpretation. We prove two structural
limitations for the convention class studied here. First, no aggregation
rule restricted to nonnegative source weights can implement the
Bayes-optimal decision when an inverted source's Bayes weight exceeds
the realized combined contribution of the remaining sources on some
message vector (the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:nonneg}); the
inverted source's own reliability strengthens the condition, the other
sources' works against it. Second, no rule assigning a single
context-independent scalar weight per source can reproduce a source's
Bayes log-odds contribution when its convention flips across
observable contexts (any stability $\rho > 0$); when $\rho > 1/2$ the
effective sign itself flips with context, and such rules fail on
decisions as well (Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar}). We complement these
with a consistency result: a Bayesian aggregator that infers each source's
latent convention from a support set concentrates on the
observational-equivalence class of the truth as evidence accumulates, so
its predictive log-odds converge to the oracle's. We introduce a finite-grid Bayesian
latent-convention aggregator and evaluate it on a benchmark of eight
involutive conventions. Bayesian aggregation consistently outperforms the
scalar baselines and matches or exceeds the learned calibrators we tested
(each in a single, untuned configuration) given identical support
information---a transformer meta-learner with $4.1\times$ the attention
baseline's parameters finishes within half a point, a small but
statistically detectable deficit, while a decoder-privileged diagnostic
nearly reaches the oracle---with the clearest margins at high convention
stability and in larger groups. In multi-agent consensus it improves group accuracy by
37.5 percentage points over naive averaging (twelve agents), finishing
within 4.3 points of an oracle. We also report calibration (negative
log-likelihood, Brier score, expected calibration error): the Bayesian
posterior scores best probabilistically but grows overconfident as
conventions stabilise, a limitation. A new
stability-boundary experiment maps when per-source decoding helps versus
hurts: at low convention stability ($\rho=0.5$) a population-average
mapping beats per-source Bayesian decoding (paired $+0.0196$ at $K=10$),
with the advantage shrinking as support grows and vanishing at $K=50$;
at $\rho \geq 0.6$ decoding wins decisively. A post-repair
validation programme adds three further evidence types: controlled
panels reproducing the mechanism with a dose--response in measured
heterogeneity; real human judgments (CrowdTruth medical relation
tasks) in which per-source behaviour measurably flips across
contexts (37\% of workers sign-flip between relation families) and a
calibrated context-decoding estimator beats the best calibrated
scalar on accuracy, log loss and calibration error across two
tasks; and an adversarial suite pricing the assumptions---under a
convention switch between support and query, every support-based
method collapses to chance. On CIFAR-10H, majority vote is already
near ceiling and source modelling gains nothing---a boundary
condition. We make no claim about multi-agent communication in
general.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{multi-agent systems, source calibration, trust aggregation,
latent-variable inference, collective decision-making}
