\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Trust and reputation in multi-agent systems}

The dominant paradigm for handling source differences is trust and
reputation, and for this lineage the scalar characterization is fair.
TRAVOS~\cite{teacy2006} estimates the probability that a source provides
accurate information from its track record; the Beta reputation
system~\cite{josang2001} maintains Bayesian posteriors over
trustworthiness; FIRE~\cite{huynh2006fire}, REGRET~\cite{sabater2002regret},
and BLADE~\cite{regan2006blade} extend the idea to witness reputation,
social structure, and learned accuracy models. Each of these models, in
the end, \emph{how often} a source is right. That is the gap we work in:
a source can be maximally reliable under every one of these frameworks
and still be unreadable, because reliability and convention are different
things.

\subsection{Opinion dynamics and social learning}

{\sloppy
Classical opinion dynamics, DeGroot~\cite{degroot1974} and
Friedkin--Johnsen~\cite{friedkin1999}, aggregate through nonnegative
influence weights: exactly the class our Theorem~\ref{thm:nonneg} rules
out for inverted conventions. Bayesian social learning~\cite{acemoglu2011}
and naive learning in networks~\cite{golub2010} ask when networks
converge to the truth, but take the mapping from state to signal as fixed
and common knowledge. We study what happens when that mapping is private,
heterogeneous, and has to be inferred.
\par}

\subsection{Crowdsourcing, truth discovery, and label noise}

The closest technical relatives of our model live here, and honesty
requires placing the model inside that lineage rather than beside it.
The founding model is Dawid and Skene's~\cite{dawid1979}: each observer
is characterised by a full confusion matrix, estimated by EM --- there
is no scalar weight anywhere in it. The lineage that followed is a
sequence of structured per-annotator response models: GLAD~\cite{whitehill2009glad}
(per-annotator expertise interacting with item difficulty), Raykar
et al.~\cite{raykar2010} (per-annotator sensitivity and specificity),
Raykar and Yu~\cite{raykar2012} (who note that annotators with
sensitivity plus specificity below one are negatively informative and
can be reversed by EM --- prior art, in the context-free case, for our
inverted sources), IBCC~\cite{kim2012ibcc} (Bayesian classifier
combination with Dirichlet priors over the confusion matrices), and,
inside truth discovery proper, Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2012bayesian} (a
Bayesian generative model with distinct per-source false-positive and
false-negative rates), alongside the truth-discovery methods
of~\cite{dong2009,yin2008,pasternack2011}. The label-noise literature
makes the same object central: a per-class transition matrix, estimated
or inverted to correct the loss~\cite{natarajan2013,patrini2017}.

Per (source, context), our $(c,\rho,r)$ model induces exactly a binary
symmetric channel --- a strict special case of one such transition
matrix, as Section~\ref{sec:formal} derives. We claim no new model
class, and the scalar-weight characterization of our introduction
applies to the trust lineage above and to weight-based opinion
dynamics, not to this response-model literature. What that literature
does not do is threefold. First, none of its response models is
conditioned on an \emph{observable context} that changes the mapping
itself: the confusion matrix is a property of the annotator, not of the
(annotator, context) pair. Second, none ties the per-context channels
through a finite family of mappings shared across sources --- the tying
that makes estimation from $K=10$ labelled examples per source possible
here, and a restriction a reader should price in. Third, none proves an
impossibility statement --- that context-independent scalar aggregation
cannot represent the Bayes rule once the mapping varies with observable
context (Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar}) --- or characterizes the stability
boundary at which per-source decoding stops paying (Experiment~I). Our
contribution is that framing, the cross-context tying as a small-$K$
inductive bias, and the boundary --- not a new response model. Truth
discovery asks who to trust. We are asking what they meant, in contexts
where what they meant changes with the context.

\subsection{Robust and adversarial aggregation}

Byzantine-tolerant aggregation~\cite{blanchard2017} and
Byzantine-robust distributed learning~\cite{yin2018} handle sources that
actively try to corrupt the outcome. Our setting is different: nobody is
attacking anyone. The sources are honest and often highly reliable; they
simply speak in different conventions, so there is no attacker to detect.
What remains is a decoding problem, and reliability scores cannot see it.

\subsection{Signaling, conventions, and pragmatics}

The idea that a fixed signal can carry opposite conventional meanings is
classical. Lewis's analysis of convention~\cite{lewis1969} and Skyrms's
evolutionary account~\cite{skyrms1996} study signaling systems in which
the same signal is associated with different states under different
equilibria --- our direct and inverted mappings are the two-element
case --- and cheap-talk theory~\cite{crawford1982} studies senders whose
messages must be decoded through known biases. The framing of our model
also grew out of partial information
decomposition~\cite{williams2010,bertschinger2014}: does source identity
carry information beyond message content? What survived is
decision-theoretic --- our theorems target decision rules directly,
since statistically real information ($I(X;S\mid M) > 0$) need not imply
decision value. The intuition is old in pragmatics: Gricean
implicature~\cite{grice1975} and relevance theory~\cite{sperber1995}
treat speaker identity and context as constitutive of meaning; our
conventions are a minimal formal version of that insight.

\subsection{Multi-agent LLM systems}

Recent work on LLM agents has explored debate protocols~\cite{du2024debate,
liang2024debate}, round-table deliberation~\cite{chen2024reconcile},
multi-agent evaluation~\cite{chan2024chateval}, and layered aggregation of
proposals~\cite{wang2024moa}. These frameworks model source differences
through confidence scores, voting, or an aggregator model's judgement, and
empirical studies find debate gains sensitive to protocol design and not
always robust over simpler baselines~\cite{smit2024mad}. What they
generally do not model is the possibility that agents encode readings
through source-specific, context-dependent conventions. Our contribution
relative to this literature is the impossibility results:
Theorems~\ref{thm:nonneg} and~\ref{thm:scalar} show that when the mapping
from readings to messages itself varies by source and context, the thing
to learn is a decoder, not a weight.
