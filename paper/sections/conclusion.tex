\section{Conclusion}

Scalar reliability weighting cannot implement the Bayes rule in two
regimes: nonnegative weighting cannot invert a source (Theorem~1), and no
context-independent scalar can reproduce a source's context-varying
Bayes log-odds contribution --- failing on decisions as well once
stability exceeds $1/2$, where the effective sign itself flips with
context (Theorem~2). The way out is to decode: our
finite-grid Bayesian latent-convention aggregator outperforms the scalar
baselines, matches or exceeds the learned calibrators we tested, and
reaches within 4.3 points of the oracle at $N=12$---37.5 points over
naive averaging. It survives candidate-set misspecification gracefully
and is not disproportionately hurt by correlated source errors. The
independent validation programme (Section~\ref{sec:validation})
carries the mechanism to real human judgments: where workers'
behaviour measurably flips across recorded contexts, a calibrated
context decoder beats the best calibrated scalar on accuracy, log
loss and calibration across two tasks, and the adversarial suite
marks the boundary---no edge when context only modulates
reliability, and collapse for every support-based method when
conventions switch between support and query. But
decoding is not always worth its estimation cost: when conventions are
unstable ($\rho=0.5$) a population-average mapping beats per-source
decoding, with the advantage vanishing as support grows
(Experiment~I)---a sharp, prescriptive boundary on when to decode. Its
probabilities are well scored but overconfident at high convention
stability. Source
identity is not a reliability parameter bolted onto a message; it is part
of the evidence-to-decision mapping.
