\section{Introduction}

Consider a deceptively simple sentence: \emph{``The bridge is safe.''}
Heard from a structural engineer, it licenses crossing. Heard from a
five-year-old, it licenses nothing. Heard from an anonymous account, it
licenses suspicion. The message is identical; the decision it supports is
not. Something about the \emph{source} changes what the message means.

Multi-agent systems meet this problem at scale. Agents exchange beliefs,
recommendations, sensor readings, and every receiver must decide how much
weight each message deserves. A substantial class of trust and reputation
models represents source differences primarily through reliability or
trust parameters, formalised as $\hat{y} = \mathrm{sign}(\sum_s w_s X_s)$
with $w_s \geq 0$. Weighted voting, reliability-weighted averaging,
trust-weighted fusion. The nonnegative weight is everywhere. We argue that
reliability weighting is insufficient for this setting.

The failure is in how messages are interpreted. Take a source that is
perfectly reliable, its internal reading always matching the truth, but
which reports through a private convention: it flips its reading, or XORs
it with an observable context bit. Its messages are uninterpretable without
the convention, and no nonnegative weight, however carefully estimated,
repairs the aggregation. Reliability and convention are orthogonal. A
source can be maximally trustworthy and still unreadable. We call the
resulting problem \emph{convention heterogeneity}. It is distinct from the
reliability heterogeneity the literature has studied.

By \emph{provenance-aware} aggregation we mean rules that condition on
source identity. The decision-theoretic value of provenance is the change
in decision value from knowing who sent the message: with the source
known, the receiver can decode through that source's convention; without
it, the message may be uninterpretable. Our impossibility theorems characterise regimes where source
identity carries this decision value yet no scalar reliability rule can
extract it, and our aggregator is a constructive method for extracting it.

We make five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We formalise the problem as a generative latent-variable model
in which source identity is part of the process that generates each
message, with
involutive conventions, a stability parameter, and reliability kept
strictly separate.
\item On the impossibility side, nonnegative source weights cannot
implement the Bayes-optimal rule under inverted conventions whenever the
inverted source's weight dominates the rest (the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:nonneg}).
\item We prove that no context-independent scalar weight can
reproduce a source's Bayes log-odds contribution under
context-dependent conventions at any stability $\rho > 0$, and that
for $\rho > 1/2$ --- where the source's effective sign flips with
context --- such rules also fail to implement the Bayes decision
(Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar}).
\item A Bayesian aggregator that infers each source's latent convention
concentrates on the observational-equivalence class of the truth as
evidence accumulates (Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency}).
\item Finally, the empirical piece: a finite-grid Bayesian
latent-convention aggregator that infers each source's convention,
stability, and reliability from a labelled support set, evaluated on a
benchmark of eight involutive conventions in nine lettered experiments
(A--I), an LLM-communication benchmark, and a real-data study.
\end{enumerate}
The Bayesian aggregator consistently outperforms the scalar baselines
under the synthetic benchmark, with the clearest margins at high
convention stability and in larger groups, and matches or exceeds the
learned calibrators we tested given identical support information: a
transformer meta-learner finishes within half a point of it, and only a
diagnostic with privileged decoded features nearly reaches the oracle.
(Bayes leads the learned MetaAttn at every group size tested, by
1.4--3.5 points; see \S6.7.)

Two scope notes. The benchmark is synthetic, the
convention family finite, the statistics exploratory: Experiment~A uses
100 seeds; Experiments~B, C, E, and I use 30; Experiments~D, F, G, and H
use 10. The implemented posterior grid covers stability
$\rho \in \{0.5, \ldots, 1.0\}$ only, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency}'s on-grid hypothesis --- and every
empirical number --- should be read with that restriction. Our
claim is conditional but sharp: reliability and communication conventions
are distinct latent variables, and aggregation methods that model only
reliability cannot recover Bayes-optimal decisions when the convention
changes what a message means.
