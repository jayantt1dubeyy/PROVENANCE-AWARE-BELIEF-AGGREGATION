\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Benchmark design}

The benchmark uses eight involutive conventions over two context bits
$(a_1, a_2)$: identity, NOT, and six XOR-with-context variants (full table
in Appendix~\ref{app:deferred-exp}). Each method receives a support set of
$K$ labelled (message, context, truth) triples per source and is evaluated
on held-out queries. Experiment~A uses 100 seeds; Experiments~B, C, E,
and I use 30; Experiments~D, F, G, and H use 10 seeds (0--9);
and the LLM benchmark uses 20 trials. Unless a caption says otherwise
we report mean $\pm$ 95\% CI (t-interval over seeds or trials); the
Experiment~D and F tables report $\pm 1$ std, as their captions state.
The statistics are exploratory: enough
to separate stable gaps from noise, not confirmatory tests.



\textbf{Baselines.} \emph{NoSupport} (content-only); \emph{UnsignedScalar}
(nonnegative weight, testing Theorem~\ref{thm:nonneg});
\emph{SignedScalar} (signed weight, testing Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar});
\emph{ContextConditioned} (Laplace context-matching);
\emph{CtxLogReg} (per-source context-dependent logistic weights);
\emph{MetaMean} and \emph{MetaAttn} (DeepSets-style neural calibrators);
\emph{DeepAttn} (wider attention decoder, $d=256$);
\emph{TransformerMeta} (self-attention meta-learner over the support set);
\emph{ContextMatchFreq} (nonparametric context-matching fallback;
named \texttt{BetaBinomial} in the code and in earlier drafts);
\emph{LogOddsScalar} (per-source log-odds scalar; \S6.4);
\emph{Bayes} (the finite-grid aggregator of Section~\ref{sec:method}, with
\emph{BayesUniv}/\emph{BayesFam} variants in the K-sweep);
\emph{NaiveMean} and \emph{MajorityVote} (uncalibrated group rules); and the
\emph{Oracle} (true $(c_s,\rho_s,r_s)$, Bayes-optimal rule).

At $\rho=0.5$ the picture is mixed, and precision about what vanishes
matters. \textsc{NoSupport} --- which ignores the \emph{support set},
while still consuming each query's message and contexts --- beats Bayes
($0.6485 \pm 0.0029$ vs.\ $0.6317 \pm 0.0023$; paired
$\Delta = +0.0169 \pm 0.0028$, $d_z = +1.19$). The messages are not
empty at $\rho=0.5$: in contexts where a source's convention does not
flip, a message still agrees with the truth with probability $r$; only
in flipped contexts does the agreement probability collapse to $1/2$
(Section~\ref{sec:formal}). What vanishes at this support size is
per-source \emph{decodability}: the population-average mapping of
\S6.3 exploits the residual signal without paying the per-source
estimation cost, and wins on bias--variance grounds; \S6.3 maps the
crossover directly. As $\rho \to 1$ the
picture changes: NoSupport collapses toward chance ($0.4831$), unsigned
and signed scalars stall, while the Bayesian aggregator climbs
monotonically to $0.7576 \pm 0.0021$ and the neural calibrators partially
recover ($0.7012$--$0.7170$). The price of ignoring conventions grows
with their stability. The nonparametric
\textsc{ContextMatchFreq} trails Bayes at every $\rho$ (at $\rho=1.0$:
$0.6763 \pm 0.0020$, paired $\Delta = -0.0812 \pm 0.0020$, $d_z = -8.10$):
flexibility without the generative structure does not recover the lost
accuracy. (A comparability note: \textsc{NoSupport} here and
\textsc{ContentOnly} in the \S6.4 ablation are the same rule family
measured in different runs --- this sweep trains on streams with
$\rho \in (0.5, 1.0)$, the ablation on $\rho \in (0.6, 1.0)$ --- so
their absolute levels are not directly comparable; the analytic
population mapping of \S6.3, which involves no training, matches
\textsc{NoSupport} at $\rho=0.5$ ($0.6496$ vs.\ $0.6485$).)
The per-query expected reliability ceiling under full convention
stability is $\mathbb{E}[r] = 0.825$ even for the oracle; the consensus
experiment (\S6.7) shows how independent sources combine to lift accuracy
far above that ceiling.

\subsection{The stability boundary: when decoding hurts}

The $\rho=0.5$ boundary condition in the stability sweep raises a
prescriptive question: when conventions are only weakly stable, is
per-source decoding ever worth its estimation cost? We characterise the
boundary directly. Define the \emph{population mapping} as the
Bayes-optimal content-only rule: for each $(m, a_1, a_2)$ predict
$\arg\max_y P(Y=y \mid m, a_1, a_2)$ where the probability averages over
the training population ($c$ uniform over eight conventions,
$\rho \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0.5, 1)$, $r \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0.7,
0.95)$). This is the analytic equivalent of what \textsc{NoSupport}
learns; it matches the banked \textsc{NoSupport} accuracy at $\rho=0.5$
($0.6496$ vs.\ $0.6485$) without any training. The comparison is then
clean: per-source Bayesian decoding (which estimates a convention per
source from $K$ support triples) versus applying the population-average
mapping (which estimates nothing per source).

Figure~\ref{fig:boundary-lines} shows the crossover at $K=10$ is sharp.
At $\rho=0.5$ the population mapping beats per-source Bayes by
$+0.0196$ (paired 95\% CI $[+0.0151, +0.0241]$, $d_z = +1.63$): estimating
a per-source convention from $K=10$ examples adds estimation noise that
outweighs the decodable signal at this stability. At $\rho=0.6$ the ordering already
flips ($-0.0174$, CI $[-0.0214, -0.0134]$, $d_z = -1.64$), and the
decoding margin grows rapidly with stability ($-0.2803$ at $\rho=1.0$,
$d_z = -12.74$). The heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:boundary-heatmap}) shows
the same pattern across support sizes: the population mapping wins only
in the low-stability regime, and its advantage shrinks as $K$ grows.
At $\rho=0.5$ the paired gain falls from $+0.0286$ at $K=2$ to $+0.0196$
at $K=10$, $+0.0065$ at $K=20$, and vanishes at $K=50$ ($+0.0001$, CI
$[-0.0034, +0.0037]$): with enough per-source data, decoding catches up
even when conventions are unstable.

The practical rule is therefore conditional on both stability and data:
decode per-source when conventions are at all stable ($\rho \gtrsim
0.6$) or support is ample; fall back to the population mapping when
conventions are unstable \emph{and} support is scarce. The boundary is
not a failure of decoding but a bias--variance tradeoff: per-source
convention inference is high-variance, and when the signal is weak the
variance dominates.

A natural refinement is to \emph{select} per source: decode when a
stability test on the source's own support set passes, else fall back
to the population mapping. We ran exactly that selector (30 seeds,
$K=10$; it chooses per-source Bayes for a fraction of sources rising
from $0.60$ at $\rho=0.5$ to $0.95$ at $\rho=1.0$). It does not beat
the better fixed rule in either regime: at $\rho=0.5$ it scores
$0.640$ in its own runs, between per-source Bayes ($0.631$) and the
population mapping ($0.652$); at $\rho=1.0$ it scores $0.749$ against
Bayes's $0.758$. Selection on the same scarce support data does not
come free, so the boundary above is stated for the two fixed rules.

by the theorems (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}):
content-only near chance, unsigned scalar low, signed scalar at $0.6207$,
context-conditioned scalar at $0.7350$, the attention calibrator at

{\sloppy
The table reads as a hierarchy that mirrors the theory: raw messages near
chance, unsigned scalars low (Theorem~\ref{thm:nonneg}), signed scalars at
$0.6207$, context-conditioned scalars at $0.7350$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar}),
full latent-convention inference at $0.7590$, and the oracle at $0.8263$.
Two notes. First, our initial \textsc{ContextConditioned} baseline
silently collapsed to \textsc{SignedScalar}; we reimplemented it with
Laplace-smoothed context-matching support agreement. Second, the
diagnostic \textsc{MetaAttn+Dec} variants receive the true-convention-decoded
bit at inference and reach the oracle ceiling ($0.8240$ vs.\ $0.8263$), so
the learned calibrator's remaining gap is representational, not a
capacity problem. Finally, the hand-crafted
\textsc{ContextConditioned} ($0.7350 \pm 0.0048$) and the higher-capacity
\textsc{AttentionCalibrator} ($0.7238 \pm 0.0053$) have overlapping 95\%
CIs, so the observed point-estimate ordering is not clearly separated at
this sample size.

A disclosure about the scalar baselines' functional form:
\textsc{UnsignedScalar} and \textsc{SignedScalar} as implemented form
a one-parameter sub-family --- each source enters through its support
agreement rate, scaled by a single learned global multiplier (linear in
the agreement rate, not its log-odds), signed in the second case. The
strongest signed member of the richer per-source log-odds family ---
weight $\log(\hat p/(1-\hat p))$ per source, parameter-free --- is
decision-identical to \textsc{SignedScalar} on all 30 seeds (both
$0.6207 \pm 0.0041$), so the scalar-class conclusion does not rest on
the restricted form.

We also tested stronger learned baselines, since a sufficiently
expressive source${}\times{}$context meta-learner might match Bayes
without generative machinery.
\textsc{DeepAttn}, a wider attention decoder ($d=256$), reaches
$0.7299 \pm 0.0040$---no better than the smaller \textsc{MetaAttn}, so
capacity is not the bottleneck. \textsc{TransformerMeta}, a
self-attention meta-learner over the support set ($104{,}834$
parameters, against \textsc{MetaAttn}'s $25{,}667$ at the same width
$d=64$ --- not capacity-matched), reaches $0.7544 \pm 0.0047$. The
paired gap to Bayes is small but statistically detectable
($\Delta = -0.0046$, 95\% CI $[-0.0070, -0.0022]$, $d_z = -0.71$): we
report it as a half-point deficit, not a tie, applying the same
paired-CI standard here as to the smaller gaps in this paper. A
sufficiently expressive sequence model can nearly match the Bayesian
decoder here, but none of the learned methods we tried beats it.
\textsc{ContextMatchFreq}, a nonparametric context-matching fallback,
trails badly at $0.6780 \pm 0.0036$ (paired $\Delta = -0.0810
\pm 0.0036$, $d_z = -8.30$): flexibility without the generative structure
does not recover the lost accuracy. The decoder structure does the work,
not capacity alone.

\textsc{CtxLogReg} tests the same objection from the discriminative side
(10-seed run): per-source
logistic regression on the same $K=10$ support set, with the logit a
context-dependent weight on the message,
$w_0 + m\,(v_0 + v_1a_1 + v_2a_2 + v_{12}a_1a_2)$, which nests
\textsc{ContextConditioned}'s per-context sign and can express any of the
eight conventions' decoding maps. It reaches only $0.637 \pm 0.007$,
below \textsc{ContextConditioned} and Bayes with non-overlapping CIs.
The result is not a regularisation artefact ($\lambda = 0.01$ and $0.1$
agree at $K=10$). Accuracy does rise with support in a dedicated sweep
($\lambda = 0.1$, 10 seeds): $0.534$ at $K=2$, $0.590$ at $K=5$,
$0.637$ at $K=10$, $0.677$ at $K=20$, $0.713$ at $K=50$ --- more data
helps the discriminative fit, without closing the gap to the structured
methods at matched $K$. At
$K=10$, expressiveness without the right inductive bias does not help.

A further ablation isolates \emph{why} Bayes beats the scalar baselines,
and answers the paper's central empirical question: does posterior
uncertainty matter beyond point-estimate decoding? We decompose the gap
on the Experiment~B streams (30 seeds; all comparisons paired; an
earlier 10-seed sensitivity run gives the same ordering). The
posterior's own MAP plug-in --- decide with the oracle log-odds formula
at the grid posterior's MAP cell --- reaches $0.7530 \pm 0.0048$: full
posterior averaging is worth only $0.6$ points over the posterior's own
point estimate (paired $\Delta = +0.0060$, 95\% CI $[+0.0031, +0.0090]$).
A posterior-mean plug-in (posterior means of $\rho, r$ under the MAP
convention) does slightly worse, $0.7494 \pm 0.0052$.
\textsc{EMDecoder}, which fits the same generative model by EM and
decides with the MAP convention, reaches only $0.6870 \pm 0.0054$ ---
and the decomposition shows the deficit is mostly not about point
estimates at all. \textsc{EMDecoder}'s decision rule drops the fitted
stability $\hat\rho$ from the plug-in weight; restoring it (the exact
oracle formula at EM's estimates) recovers $0.7343 \pm 0.0038$, a paired
gain of $4.7$ points (95\% CI $[+0.0438, +0.0508]$) attributable to the
rule. The remainder is fit quality: EM's MAP convention is correct for
$0.393 \pm 0.014$ of sources at $K=10$ (5,400 sources), against
$0.640 \pm 0.014$ for the grid posterior's MAP on the same streams.
Bayes's $7.2$-point margin over \textsc{EMDecoder} therefore decomposes
as $\approx 4.7$ points of decision rule, $\approx 1.9$ points of
convention recovery, and $\approx 0.6$ points of posterior averaging.
Explicit context conditioning recovers much of the lost performance;
Bayesian inference over the latent convention adds the rest, mostly by
recovering conventions better than EM's point fit does.
\par}

\subsection{Support-size scaling on held-out conventions}

(support sets are nonempty, as the neural encoders require).
``Held-out'' means the following. The neural calibrators are trained on
sources using conventions $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ and evaluated on sources using
conventions $\{4, 5, 6, 7\}$, with $K$ labelled support examples per
source available at test time for calibration. \textsc{BayesUniv}
considers all eight conventions as candidates.
\textsc{BayesFam} restricts its candidate set to $\{4, 5, 6, 7\}$: it

\subsection{Reliability heterogeneity}

$\rho \in [0.99, 1.0]$. The three regimes are
\emph{mean-matched}: homogeneous $r \in [0.79, 0.81]$, moderate $r \in
[0.70, 0.90]$, heterogeneous $r \in [0.65, 0.95]$, all with
$\mathbb{E}[r] = 0.80$. This matching matters: with the means matched, any remaining difference across
regimes is attributable to the \emph{spread} of reliabilities alone.

\subsection{Multi-agent consensus}

$N$ agents observe a shared binary truth, each with its own $(c, \rho, r)$
sampled i.i.d.\ as follows:
$c_s \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(\mathcal{C})$,
$\rho_s \sim \mathrm{Uniform}([0.7, 1.0])$,
$r_s \sim \mathrm{Uniform}([0.7, 0.95])$.
The sampling distribution does not depend on $N$; realized diversity
increases with $N$ because more i.i.d.\ draws are combined.

grows the naive-to-calibrated gap widens: more agents help only if you
can decode them.

\subsection{Calibration}

Accuracy is only half the story: the Bayesian aggregator returns
probabilities, so the re-run experiments also record negative
log-likelihood, Brier score, and expected calibration error (ECE, 10
equal-width bins). In the stability sweep (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}),
Bayes has the best NLL and Brier score at every $\rho$ (at $\rho=1.0$:
NLL $0.5480 \pm 0.0018$, Brier $0.1820 \pm 0.0008$). Its ECE, however,
rises with stability, from $0.0170 \pm 0.0012$ at $\rho=0.5$ to $0.0836
\pm 0.0020$ at $\rho=1.0$: as conventions stabilise, the posterior
concentrates and grows overconfident. At $\rho=1.0$ the learned
\textsc{MetaAttn} is actually the better-calibrated of the two (ECE
$0.0587 \pm 0.0024$)---the Bayesian posterior's overconfidence at high
stability is a genuine weakness, not shared by the discriminative
calibrator.

In the ablation (Experiment~B) the oracle is the best calibrated by far
(ECE $0.0105 \pm 0.0012$), with Bayes at $0.0837 \pm 0.0044$. In
consensus, the two methods' calibration moves in opposite directions as
the group grows: Bayes's ECE falls from $0.1359 \pm 0.0108$ at $N=2$ to
$0.0614 \pm 0.0044$ at $N=12$---more independent sources genuinely
sharpen the posterior---while \textsc{MetaAttn}'s ECE rises steeply from
$0.1443 \pm 0.0112$ to $0.3389 \pm 0.0160$. The learned aggregator's
probabilities degrade as the group grows even while its accuracy
improves. Calibration is thus a second axis on which the methods differ,
and a real limitation of the current Bayesian aggregator: without
recalibration, its probabilities should not be taken at face value at
high convention stability.

strong inside its assumed family; this experiment bounds the cost of

\subsection{Correlated sources: breaking the independence assumption}

Every result so far keeps the model correctly specified. Here it is
deliberately \emph{not} the data-generating process: the Bayesian
aggregator assumes sources are conditionally independent given the truth,
so we correlate their errors. Ten sources (five pairs) report on one
shared truth per query; within-pair query-time errors correlate at
$\approx 0.5$ (Gaussian copula, calibrated; measured $0.44$--$0.52$),
with ExpB draws otherwise ($\rho \in [0.99, 1.0]$, $r \in [0.7, 0.95]$,
$K = 10$). Support data stay independent, isolating the misspecification
to the combination step. A paired-seed independence arm is the control;
\textsc{MetaAttn} could not be re-run here (torch unavailable). Correlation
costs every method about a point, \emph{including the oracle}, which also
sums independent log-odds: Bayes $0.971 \to 0.958$,
\textsc{ContextConditioned} $0.962 \to 0.950$,
\textsc{SignedScalar} $0.847 \to 0.844$, naive $0.449 \to 0.457$, Oracle
$0.994 \to 0.983$ (mean $\pm$ 95\% CI over 10 seeds; paired per-seed deltas
$-0.013$, $-0.012$, $-0.004$, $+0.008$, $-0.011$, all within noise of each
other). The problem itself gets harder under correlation, and Bayes is
not disproportionately hurt: the ranking Bayes ${} > {}$
\textsc{ContextConditioned} ${} > {}$ \textsc{SignedScalar} ${} > {}$
naive is preserved in both arms.

\subsection{LLM communication benchmark}

As a step beyond simulation, we instantiate the five-source population
with a real language model. Each source is an independent
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} session given one of five induced conventions: A
answers truthfully; B negates every belief; C negates exactly when a
random context flag is 1; D answers truthfully but carelessly (about 15\%
of answers flipped); E answers truthfully on science/nature questions and
negates everything else. The model is \emph{told} its private belief on
each proposition (correct with probability $0.85$), so the experiment
tests convention-following and decoding, not the model's factual
knowledge: a controlled communication benchmark, not a claim about
real-world deployment. We use 120 verifiable propositions, $K=8$ labelled
support answers per source and $Q=20$ queries per trial, over 20 trials;
the Bayesian aggregator reuses the synthetic convention family directly
on the parsed Yes/No bits. Its candidate set in the released code is
$\{0, 1, 2, 3, 8\}$, which cannot express source E's topic-conditioned
rule: E's mapping is a function of the proposition's topic, not of the
context bits the synthetic family flips on.

Decoding conventions helps enormously. We report this run as a
\emph{pre-fix-harness} result: the numbers below were banked before the
harness defects described in the caveats were found; the banked
``oracle'' was handed an inverted rule for source E and is beaten by
every decoder, so it is not an upper bound and we do not treat it as
one; and the numbers are not reproducible from the released (fixed)
code. Raw API outputs from the original run were not retained; the
released harness now saves them. Mean accuracy $\pm$ 95\% t-CI over
the 20 trials: \textsc{SignedScalar} $0.9950 \pm 0.0072$,
\textsc{ContextConditioned} $0.9900 \pm 0.0096$,
\textsc{Bayes} $0.9875 \pm 0.0104$, nominal Oracle $0.9800 \pm 0.0140$, and
\textsc{NaiveMean} $0.8175 \pm 0.0451$. Paired gains over the naive mean
are $+0.1775 \pm 0.0477$ ($d_z = 1.74$),
$+0.1725 \pm 0.0483$ ($d_z = 1.67$),
$+0.1700 \pm 0.0477$ ($d_z = 1.67$) and
$+0.1625 \pm 0.0491$ ($d_z = 1.55$) respectively: about seventeen points,
with every interval well clear of zero. Near-ceiling accuracies leave
little headroom between the decoders themselves, so this experiment
separates decoding from not decoding rather than ranking the decoders.

Two caveats. First, convention adherence (fraction of support
messages matching the induced rule) was high for A--D ($0.925$, $0.950$,
$0.938$, $0.888$), but the diagnostic for E read $0.100$. Auditing the
code showed the benchmark's expected rule for E was inverted relative to
its prompt: it checked negate-on-science where the prompt says
truthful-on-science. Under the inverted check a perfectly compliant
source would read near zero --- not exactly zero, since the model's own
errors blur the reading --- so $0.100$ is consistent with high
compliance with the prompt as written; it does not pin compliance at
any precise figure such as 90\%. The failure was in our harness, not
the model, and the mapping is fixed in the released code. Because E's
true rule was also missing from every decoder's candidate set, the
decoders solved a harder problem than the design intended; the
direction of the resulting bias on the decoder gaps is not determined,
and we do not claim the gaps are conservative. Second, the
private beliefs here are supplied, so this says nothing about extracting
beliefs the model actually holds; it validates the aggregation machinery
given convention-following speakers.

\subsection{Real human judgments}

As a check on genuine human data, we aggregate the CIFAR-10H judgments
\cite{peterson2019human}: roughly 51 category labels for each of the
10,000 CIFAR-10 test images from 2,571 annotators (514,200 judgments
after attention checks are removed; every annotator labels exactly 200
images, and all 2,571 workers had estimation-half observations). We split
the images into disjoint estimation and evaluation halves of 5,000, fit
per-worker confusion matrices on the estimation half with the CIFAR-10
gold labels, and aggregate each evaluation image's annotations to predict
its gold label. Five rules are compared: \textsc{MajorityVote},
accuracy-weighted voting (scalar reliability weights), a Bayesian rule
using plug-in smoothed confusion matrices (\textsc{BayesDirichlet}; no
integration over matrix uncertainty), Dawid--Skene
EM~\cite{dawid1979} warm-started from the estimation-half confusion
counts and iterated to convergence --- the label posterior is
recomputed from the current confusion matrices at every iteration
(5 iterations in the full regime, 92 in the five-judgment regime) ---
and an oracle using confusion matrices fit on all data. Mean accuracy $\pm$ 95\%
CI over the 5,000 evaluation images, with paired deltas against majority
vote reported below:

{\small

\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Method & $\sim$51 judgments & 5 judgments \\
\midrule
\textsc{MajorityVote} & $0.9924 \pm 0.0024$ & $0.9844 \pm 0.0034$ \\
\textsc{AccuracyWeighted} & $0.9926 \pm 0.0024$ & $0.9846 \pm 0.0034$ \\
\textsc{BayesDirichlet} & $0.9930 \pm 0.0023$ & $0.9842 \pm 0.0035$ \\
\textsc{DawidSkene} & $0.9928 \pm 0.0023$ & $0.9850 \pm 0.0034$ \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
}

Paired deltas against majority vote (mean $\pm$ 95\% CI) are tiny: with all
judgments, $+0.0002 \pm 0.0004$ (accuracy-weighted), $+0.0006 \pm 0.0010$
(BayesDirichlet), $+0.0004 \pm 0.0008$ (DawidSkene), and $+0.0036 \pm 0.0017$
(oracle). With five judgments the deltas are $+0.0002 \pm 0.0012$,
$-0.0002 \pm 0.0014$, $+0.0006 \pm 0.0014$, and $+0.0034 \pm 0.0017$.

This is nearly a null result, and that is
informative. CIFAR-10H annotators are individually strong (mean accuracy
$0.949$), so majority vote already reaches $0.9924$, and no
source-modelling method adds anything measurable: every paired interval
covers zero. Even the oracle, with perfect knowledge of every worker,
gains only about a third of a point. The five-judgment regime---the
realistic crowdsourcing budget---tells the same story at $0.9844$.
This is the boundary condition the thesis predicts rather than a
failure of it: the large synthetic gains (Experiment~E's $+37.5$ points
over naive averaging) come from heterogeneous, unreliable sources, and
these annotators are neither. The experiment's value is in showing the
machinery does not manufacture an advantage where none exists.

Two scope notes. First, this tests only the reliability-heterogeneity
half of the thesis---workers differ in accuracy and confusion patterns,
but everyone answers in the same ten-way format, so there is nothing to
decode. The convention-encoding half is not exercised by this dataset;
it is tested on real data in Section~\ref{sec:validation}. Second, the gold labels here are the CIFAR-10 test
labels; on genuinely ambiguous images the ``correct'' answer is itself
contested, which is one more reason not to over-read small gaps.

An audit-repair note: every dataset statistic quoted in this section
(514,200 judgments; 2,571 annotators; exactly 200 judgments per
annotator; mean worker accuracy $0.949$, computed as $0.9487$) was
recomputed from the raw CIFAR-10H release for this revision, and the
Dawid--Skene baseline was re-implemented as genuine iterated EM (an
earlier released version computed the label posterior once and never
iterated it). The repair changes only the five-judgment
\textsc{DawidSkene} figure ($0.9846 \to 0.9850$); the full-regime
predictions are identical, and no conclusion in this section moves.

\subsection{Independent validation: mechanism, real conventions, failure map}
\label{sec:validation}

The experiments above are the paper's own. As a post-repair check we
ran a separate validation programme (PVI-REAL; code and outputs in
\texttt{code/pvi\_real}, \texttt{results/pvi\_real}) against
hypotheses fixed before the runs: (H1)~real sources' behaviour varies
by context, including sign flips; (H2)~the scalar failure grows with
measured heterogeneity; (H3)~the decoder recovers conventions from
support with quantifiable sample efficiency; (H4)~the advantage is
mediated by source identity and context (ablations must destroy it);
(H5)~the effect survives on real data.

\paragraph{Mechanism under controlled panels.}
We re-ran the paper's generative machinery as a virtual panel study:
five regimes (low heterogeneity; fixed inversion; context
conventions; a boundary mix; emergent drifting conventions), 40
panels of six sources per regime, 40 shared query items, support
nested at $K \in \{0,2,5,10,20,40\}$, with Majority, both scalars,
ContextMatchFreq, a per-context transition-matrix method
(PerContextTM), the grid Bayes of Section~5, and an oracle. Controls
behave as the theory requires: at low heterogeneity the scalar is
best (log loss $0.094$ vs.\ the decoder's $0.117$) and under fixed
inversion it wins outright ($0.154$/$0.938$), while Majority
collapses ($0.614$ accuracy). Where conventions vary by context the
decoder's advantage is large and dose-responsive: at $K{=}20$, log
loss $0.269$ vs.\ the scalar's $0.488$ and accuracy $0.886$ vs.\
$0.781$; in the emergent regime, $0.327$/$0.848$ vs.\ $0.687$/$0.666$.
The panel-level advantage regresses on measured heterogeneity with
slope $0.73$ $[0.65, 0.81]$, and the slope survives within the
context regime alone ($1.14$ $[0.58, 1.60]$), so it is not an artefact
of contrasting regimes. Shuffling source identities destroys $366\%$
of the advantage (the decoder falls \emph{below} the scalar),
shuffling contexts $215\%$, and a placebo context bit exactly the
$75\%$ attributable to the real second context (H4). The decoder
closes $80\%$ (context) to $91\%$ (emergent) of the scalar-to-oracle
log-loss gap by $K{=}40$, reaches the scalar's $K{=}40$ log loss by
$K{=}5$ in the context regime, and loses to the scalar in only 3 of
40 context panels---the lowest-heterogeneity ones. A power analysis
on these panels sizes a human replication at $\approx$15 panels per
arm. This arm validates estimator behaviour and internal
consistency; by construction it cannot validate the shape of real
conventions.

\paragraph{Real conventions in human judgments (CrowdTruth).}
The CIFAR-10H study above cannot test the convention half of the
thesis: its contexts are unrecorded and its format uniform. The
CrowdTruth medical relation corpus~\cite{crowdtruthmedical} records
both: crowd workers judged candidate cause/treat relations in
medical sentences, with expert labels, across two microtasks
(relation extraction, RelEx; direction construction, RelDir) and
two annotation channels. Taking contexts as (relation family
$\times$ channel) and a labelled support/query split of items, the
premise is directly measurable (H1): among workers with $\geq$15
judgments in each family, the median absolute cross-family accuracy
gap is $0.155$, and $37\%$ are above chance in one family and below
it in the other---real sources do flip by context. We evaluate the
paper's decoding principle in its scalable form, a
context-conditioned log-odds aggregator whose per-(source, context)
weights are shrunk toward the source's scalar weight, with one
temperature per method fitted by cross-fitting on the support only
(no query data is touched in fitting); the full grid Bayes is not
applicable here because these contexts are item families, not the
involutive family of Section~3. On RelEx (5,564 judgments, 497
items, 67 workers, 20 splits), the calibrated context decoder beats
the identically calibrated scalar on every metric: log loss $0.395$
vs.\ $0.430$ (paired advantage $+0.035$ $[0.028, 0.041]$), accuracy
$0.830$ vs.\ $0.807$ ($+2.3$ points $[1.7, 3.0]$), calibration error
$0.087$ vs.\ $0.094$; Majority scores $0.719$/$0.682$ and
Dawid--Skene $1.62$/$0.774$. Uncalibrated, both weighted methods are
severely overconfident (ECE $\approx 0.145$) and the context method
\emph{loses} on log loss---calibration is part of the method, not a
patch. Effects are context-specific and are reported per context as
a matter of policy: the RelEx gain is carried by treat items
($+4.0$ $[3.3, 4.6]$) with cause items null ($-0.2$ $[-1.3, +1.0]$).
On RelDir (4,481 judgments, 751 items, 77 workers) the same frozen
pipeline replicates with a larger effect---log-loss advantage
$+0.101$ $[0.098, 0.105]$, accuracy $+3.4$ points $[2.6, 4.2]$,
gains in both families (cause $+4.6$ $[3.5, 5.7]$, treat $+2.0$
$[1.0, 2.9]$)---while Dawid--Skene collapses below chance
($0.374$). Two scope notes: both tasks share one dataset family and
domain (medical text), so a different-domain replication is still
owed; and ChaosNLI, an obvious candidate, is unusable for any
per-source study---its release contains no annotator identifiers,
which we verified item by item.

\paragraph{Adversarial failure map.}
Four regimes probe the assumptions directly (40 panels each). (i)
\emph{Out-of-set conventions}: sources use the pattern
$\text{parity} = 1 - a_2$, outside the candidate set; per-cell
methods win (ContextMatchFreq log loss $0.266$, PerContextTM
$0.258$, decoder $0.590$, scalar $0.744$)---the candidate set is
load-bearing, though misspecification degrades the decoder to
``better than scalar'' rather than to uselessness. (ii)
\emph{Reliability-only context}: reliability varies by context with
no flips; Majority is near-oracle ($0.348$/$0.844$) and context
machinery only adds variance---the thesis's regime is sign-changing
conventions, and outside it there is nothing to win. (iii)
\emph{Drift}: stability decays from $0.95$ (support) to $0.60$
(query); the decoder remains best on accuracy but at $K{=}40$ its
log loss ($0.461$) slips behind ContextMatchFreq ($0.421$)---stale
support buys confidence in parameters that have moved. (iv)
\emph{Mimic-then-flip}: sources behave as identity on support and
flip on queries; every support-based method collapses to chance
accuracy with log loss $1.3$--$2.9$, against a query-truth oracle at
$0.089$/$0.967$. The stationarity assumption---conventions persist
from support to query---is thus not a caveat but a priced
precondition of the entire approach, ours included.
